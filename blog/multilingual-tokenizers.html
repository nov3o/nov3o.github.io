How a multilingual tokenizer should be done

Byte-level BPE starts every language from the same 256 bytes. That sounds fair. It isn't: English starts with its alphabet already done, and everyone else starts with shrapnel.

The wasted merges

In UTF-8, only a–z (and friends) are one byte. A Russian п is two bytes, D0 BF. A Hindi क is three. A rare CJK character can be four.

BPE does eventually glue these back together. But look at what it spends to get there:

  • 1 merge per letter for 2-byte scripts (Cyrillic, Greek, Arabic, Hebrew, accented Latin).
  • 2 merges per letter for 3-byte scripts (Indic, Thai, Khmer, Ethiopic, Hangul, CJK).
  • 3 merges for 4-byte characters.

These are the most obvious merges in the whole corpus. D0 followed by BF is п, always, everywhere. There is nothing to learn. Yet the trainer counts those byte pairs across billions of words, ranks them against real subwords, and burns vocabulary slots and merge steps rediscovering a fact that is written down in the Unicode standard. For Devanagari alone that's ~160 merges before the tokenizer has learned a single Hindi word.

And it only happens if the script is frequent enough. A rare script never gets its letters rebuilt, so the model has to learn that three byte tokens mean one letter, and it can happily emit half a character.

Just preload the alphabets

The fix is boring: put every letter of every living script into the base vocabulary before the first merge. Alphabets are small. Here's roughly what each one costs (both cases, plus the diacritics/vowel signs actually in use):

The only expensive ones are the composite systems: Chinese, kanji and precomposed Hangul. Everything else in the world fits in about 3.5k tokens. With common CJK and Hangul, the whole seed is ~10k, which is a rounding error on a 150–260k vocabulary. Keep a byte fallback for the long tail and nothing is ever out of vocabulary.

Not really.

Apart from Gemma's seed, every letter in this table got there by an automatic merge, not by preloading. That's how you end up with Llama 3.1 at 0% on Telugu and 34% on Cyrillic: a Telugu or Russian user types a perfectly ordinary, well-recognized letter, and the model sees two or three loose bytes.

  • *Gemma, actually does seed. SentencePiece lists the merged pieces first and appends the seed characters after them. They exist from step zero, and that's why Gemma has the best coverage on most scripts. The catch: which characters get seeded is a frequency cut on the training data, not a design choice. Anything below the cut still falls back to raw bytes that are never merged.
  • Qwen3 gets good coverage the brute-force way: a block of rare characters bolted onto the end of the merges (ids ≈ 144k–151k).
  • Llama 3.1 has zero single-token letters in Georgian, Telugu, Myanmar, Ethiopic and Canadian syllabics.

Not just alphabets

The same goes for any domain with a fixed vocabulary. In code, def, class, import, return, for, if, else, while, #include, function, const, public static void and the common indentation runs are known in advance for every major language. A trainer that has to assemble import from i, m, p… on every run is doing the same wasted work. Seed the reserved words and standard sequences as base tokens and let the merges go to what's actually new.

What this doesn't fix

Seeding equalizes the start, not the finish: merges still go to whoever has the most data. But it stops the trainer from wasting its first moves on facts it could have been told, and it means every language begins where English always did, at its own alphabet.
